.. _`theory:nls:ci`:

Confidence Intervals for Nonlinear Least Squares
================================================

When a calibration is performed with one of Dakota's tailored nonlinear
least squares solvers (see :ref:`nls`), Dakota reports a confidence
interval for each calibrated parameter at the solution. This chapter
describes the statistical model behind these intervals, how they are
computed, and the assumptions that limit their validity. The approach
is the classical linearization (asymptotic) theory of nonlinear
regression; see :cite:p:`Seb03` for a thorough treatment and
:cite:p:`Vug07` for a comparison with alternative confidence region
techniques.

.. _`theory:nls:ci:model`:

Statistical Model
-----------------

Consider the least squares problem

.. math::

   \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x} \in \Re^p}
     f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} T_i(\mathbf{x})^2
     = \mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})^T \mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}),

where :math:`\mathbf{x}` is the vector of :math:`p` calibration
parameters and :math:`\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}) = [T_1(\mathbf{x}), \ldots,
T_n(\mathbf{x})]^T` is the vector of :math:`n` least squares terms. In
the most common case each term is a residual between a model prediction
and an observation,

.. math::

   T_i(\mathbf{x}) = R_i(\mathbf{x}) - \bar{R}_i .

To make statistical statements about :math:`\hat{\mathbf{x}}`, the
observations are assumed to be generated by the model at some unknown
true parameter value :math:`\mathbf{x}^*`, corrupted by additive noise:

.. math::

   \bar{R}_i = R_i(\mathbf{x}^*) + \epsilon_i, \qquad
   \epsilon_i \overset{iid}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
   \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n,

with common but unknown error variance :math:`\sigma^2`. Under this
model the least squares estimate :math:`\hat{\mathbf{x}}` is also the
maximum likelihood estimate of :math:`\mathbf{x}^*`.

.. _`theory:nls:ci:linearization`:

Linearization
-------------

Let

.. math::

   \mathbf{J} = \left[ \frac{\partial T_i}{\partial x_j}
     \right]_{\mathbf{x} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}} \in \Re^{n \times p}

denote the Jacobian matrix of the least squares terms with respect to
the parameters, evaluated at the solution. Near :math:`\hat{\mathbf{x}}`
the model is approximated by its first-order Taylor expansion,

.. math::

   \mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}) \approx \mathbf{T}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})
     + \mathbf{J} \, (\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}),

so locally the problem is a *linear* regression with design matrix
:math:`\mathbf{J}`. Applying linear regression theory to this
approximation gives the approximate sampling distribution of the
estimator,

.. math::

   \hat{\mathbf{x}} \;\dot\sim\; \mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{x}^*, \;
     \sigma^2 \left( \mathbf{J}^T \mathbf{J} \right)^{-1} \right).

The matrix :math:`(\mathbf{J}^T \mathbf{J})^{-1}` is the inverse of the
Gauss-Newton approximation to the Hessian of :math:`f/2`, the same
matrix used by the Gauss-Newton solution methods (see
:ref:`nls:formulations`). The approximation becomes exact as the noise
level goes to zero or the number of observations grows, and it is exact
for any :math:`n` if the model is linear in the parameters.

.. _`theory:nls:ci:variance`:

Estimating the Error Variance
-----------------------------

The error variance :math:`\sigma^2` is estimated from the residual sum
of squares at the solution:

.. math::

   \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{N_{dof}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} T_i(\hat{\mathbf{x}})^2,
   \qquad N_{dof} = n - p .

Dividing by the residual degrees of freedom :math:`N_{dof}` instead of
:math:`n` accounts for the :math:`p` parameters fitted to the data, and
makes :math:`\hat{\sigma}^2` unbiased in the linear case. (If
:math:`n = p`, Dakota uses :math:`N_{dof} = 1` to avoid dividing by
zero. Intervals computed this way have essentially no statistical
meaning.)

.. _`theory:nls:ci:intervals`:

Standard Errors and Confidence Intervals
----------------------------------------

The estimated covariance of the parameter estimates is
:math:`\hat{\sigma}^2 (\mathbf{J}^T\mathbf{J})^{-1}`, so the standard
error of the :math:`j`-th parameter is

.. math::

   SE_j = \hat{\sigma} \sqrt{ \left( \mathbf{J}^T \mathbf{J} \right)^{-1}_{jj} } .

Because :math:`\sigma^2` is estimated rather than known, the
standardized quantity :math:`(\hat{x}_j - x_j^*)/SE_j` approximately
follows a Student's t-distribution with :math:`N_{dof}` degrees of
freedom. The two-sided :math:`100(1-\alpha)\%` confidence interval for
parameter :math:`x_j` is therefore

.. math::

   \hat{x}_j \pm t_{1-\alpha/2, \, N_{dof}} \cdot SE_j ,

where :math:`t_{1-\alpha/2, \, N_{dof}}` is the :math:`1-\alpha/2`
quantile of the t-distribution. Dakota reports 95% intervals
(:math:`\alpha = 0.05`, so the quantile :math:`t_{0.975, \, N_{dof}}` is
used).

These are *individual* (marginal) intervals: each covers its own
parameter with approximately 95% probability, but the probability that
all :math:`p` intervals cover their parameters at once is generally
lower. The intervals also ignore correlation between parameter
estimates, which is described by the off-diagonal entries of
:math:`(\mathbf{J}^T\mathbf{J})^{-1}`. Joint regions, such as the
linearized ellipsoid

.. math::

   \left\{ \mathbf{x} : (\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}})^T \mathbf{J}^T
     \mathbf{J} (\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}) \le p \, \hat{\sigma}^2
     F_{1-\alpha;\, p, \, N_{dof}} \right\},

or simultaneous Bonferroni intervals (which replace :math:`\alpha` by
:math:`\alpha/p`), are alternatives that Dakota does not currently
report.

.. _`theory:nls:ci:qr`:

Numerical Computation
---------------------

Forming :math:`\mathbf{J}^T\mathbf{J}` explicitly squares the condition
number of :math:`\mathbf{J}`, which can cause significant loss of
precision when parameters are poorly identified. Instead, Dakota takes
a thin QR factorization

.. math::

   \mathbf{J} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R}, \qquad
   \mathbf{Q} \in \Re^{n \times p}, \; \mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}, \;
   \mathbf{R} \in \Re^{p \times p} \text{ upper triangular}.

Then

.. math::

   \left( \mathbf{J}^T \mathbf{J} \right)^{-1}
   = \left( \mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{Q}^T \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R} \right)^{-1}
   = \mathbf{R}^{-1} \mathbf{R}^{-T},

so the needed diagonal entries are the squared Euclidean norms of the
rows of the triangular inverse :math:`\mathbf{R}^{-1}`:

.. math::

   \left( \mathbf{J}^T \mathbf{J} \right)^{-1}_{jj}
   = \sum_{k=j}^{p} \left( \mathbf{R}^{-1} \right)_{jk}^2 .

Only :math:`\mathbf{R}` must be computed and inverted. If
:math:`\mathbf{J}` is rank deficient (for example, a parameter has no
effect on any residual, or two parameters have the same effect),
:math:`\mathbf{R}` is singular and the parameters cannot be identified
from the data. Dakota detects this only when a diagonal entry of
:math:`\mathbf{R}` is exactly zero, in which case it issues a warning and
reports no intervals. The QR factorization does not use column pivoting
or a rank tolerance, so in floating point arithmetic a rank deficient or
nearly rank deficient :math:`\mathbf{J}` usually yields small but nonzero
diagonal entries instead. Dakota then reports intervals, but they are
extremely wide or numerically meaningless. Very large intervals should
be taken as a sign that the corresponding parameters are poorly
identified, not as a reliable measure of uncertainty.

.. _`theory:nls:ci:weighting`:

Weighted, Error-Scaled, and Scaled Residuals
--------------------------------------------

The intervals are computed from the least squares terms that the solver
actually minimized. When Dakota transforms the residuals before
handing them to the solver, :math:`\mathbf{T}` and :math:`\mathbf{J}`
above refer to the transformed terms. The parameters, however, are
always in their original (native, unscaled) units, so the intervals
are reported in the user's parameter space.

**Observation error covariance.** When observation errors with
covariance :math:`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}` are given with calibration data,
Dakota computes the Cholesky factorization
:math:`\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T` and whitens the
raw residuals :math:`\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x}) -
\bar{\mathbf{R}}`:

.. math::

   \mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{L}^{-1} \mathbf{r}(\mathbf{x}),
   \qquad
   \mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L}^{-1} \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} .

For a diagonal covariance this is the familiar variance-weighted form
:math:`T_i = r_i / \sigma_i`. The minimized objective is the generalized
least squares misfit :math:`\mathbf{r}^T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{r}`,
and :math:`(\mathbf{J}^T\mathbf{J})^{-1}` becomes
:math:`(\mathbf{G}^T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{G})^{-1}`, where
:math:`\mathbf{G} = \partial\mathbf{r}/\partial\mathbf{x}`. Dakota still
estimates :math:`\hat{\sigma}^2` from the data rather than fixing it to
one. The specified covariance therefore sets the *relative* weighting of
the observations, and :math:`\hat{\sigma}^2` acts as an overall scale
factor fitted to the data. A value of :math:`\hat{\sigma}^2` far from one
suggests that the specified error magnitudes are inconsistent with the
observed misfit.

**Residual weights.** User-specified nonnegative weights :math:`w_i`
define the objective :math:`\sum_i w_i r_i^2`, which is equivalent to
least squares terms :math:`T_i = \sqrt{w_i} \, r_i` with Jacobian rows
scaled by :math:`\sqrt{w_i}`. Under the statistical model above, this
corresponds to assuming :math:`\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_i) \propto 1/w_i`.
A term with zero weight contributes nothing to the residual sum of
squares or to :math:`\mathbf{J}`, but it is still counted in :math:`n`
when computing :math:`N_{dof} = n - p`. Zero weights therefore inflate
:math:`N_{dof}`, which makes :math:`\hat{\sigma}^2` and the t quantile
too small and the intervals too narrow.

**Response scaling.** Scaling each residual by a constant :math:`c_i`
has the same effect on the intervals as a weight :math:`w_i = c_i^2`. If
every residual is scaled by the same constant :math:`c`,
:math:`\hat{\sigma}^2` is multiplied by :math:`c^2` and
:math:`(\mathbf{J}^T\mathbf{J})^{-1}` by :math:`c^{-2}`, so the
intervals do not change. This equivalence applies only to value
(multiplicative) scaling. With logarithmic response scaling the solver
minimizes the sum of squares of the log-transformed terms, and the
intervals are those of that transformed problem: :math:`\mathbf{T}` and
:math:`\mathbf{J}` are the log-scaled terms and their derivatives, and
the error model of :ref:`theory:nls:ci:model` is assumed to hold for the
log-scaled terms rather than for the original residuals.

**Parameter scaling.** If the solver works with scaled parameters
:math:`\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x})`, the chain rule converts
the Jacobian back to native parameters before the intervals are
formed:

.. math::

   \frac{\partial \mathbf{T}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} =
   \frac{\partial \mathbf{T}}{\partial \mathbf{u}}
   \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} .

.. _`theory:nls:ci:limitations`:

Assumptions and Limitations
---------------------------

The intervals are only as reliable as the assumptions behind them:

- **Linearity.** The intervals come from a local linear approximation
  at :math:`\hat{\mathbf{x}}`. For strongly nonlinear models, or when
  the noise is large compared with the curvature of the model, the true
  confidence region can be far from the symmetric interval, and its
  actual coverage can differ greatly from 95%. Profile likelihood or
  Bayesian calibration (see :ref:`uq:bayesian`) gives a more faithful
  picture in such cases.

- **Error model.** Independent, identically distributed Gaussian errors
  (after any weighting or whitening) are assumed. Model form error,
  correlated errors that are not described by the covariance, or
  heavy-tailed noise all invalidate the nominal coverage.

- **Interior optimum.** The derivation assumes :math:`\hat{\mathbf{x}}`
  is an unconstrained local minimum. The intervals ignore parameter
  bounds and other constraints. They are not truncated to the bounds,
  and they may be meaningless when a constraint is active at the
  solution.

- **Derivative accuracy.** :math:`\mathbf{J}` must be accurate.
  Intervals are not computed when the solver's internal (vendor)
  finite differencing is used. Inaccurate gradients give inaccurate
  intervals.

- **Problem size.** At least as many least squares terms as parameters
  (:math:`n \ge p`) are required, and :math:`\mathbf{J}` must have full
  column rank.

- **Multiple experiments.** With replicate experiments, :math:`n`
  counts the residuals from all experiments together, and the formulas
  above are applied to the stacked system. Dakota warns that intervals
  may be inaccurate in this case.
